I've seen the same handful of mistakes sink new dealers over and over, from both sides of the counter. Some cost a few weeks. Some cost thousands of dollars. A couple cost people their license entirely. The frustrating thing is that almost every one of them is completely avoidable if you know to watch for it.

So here's my list — the real one, drawn from applications I bounced, dealers I watched struggle, and a couple of mistakes I made myself. Learn these on my dime instead of yours.

## Mistake 1: Signing a lease before checking zoning

This is the big one. It's the mistake I mentioned in three other guides because it's *that* common and *that* expensive.

Someone finds the perfect lot — good road, right price, room for a dozen cars — and signs a lease that day out of excitement. Then they discover the parcel isn't zoned for automotive sales. Now they're paying rent on a location they legally can't use as a dealership.

**The fix is free and takes ten minutes:** call your city or county zoning office *before* you sign anything. Ask specifically whether the address permits **used motor vehicle sales.** Get the answer, ideally in writing. Retail zoning is not the same as automotive zoning. Don't assume.

## Mistake 2: Underbudgeting the whole operation

New dealers fixate on the license fee and forget the license is the *cheapest* part. Then they spend their last dollar getting licensed and have nothing left to buy inventory — or to survive the first slow month.

**The fix:** budget the *whole* thing — bond premium, insurance, location deposit, signage, office setup — and then keep a **cash reserve** on top for inventory and lean weeks. If getting licensed empties your account, you're not ready to open yet. Build the cushion first.

## Mistake 3: Getting sloppy with odometer disclosures

I cannot overstate this one. Odometer disclosure is **federally regulated**, and states enforce it hard. Every eligible sale requires an accurate odometer statement, and "I forgot" is not a defense.

New dealers treat it as a formality, scribble something, or skip it. Then an audit or a customer complaint turns a paperwork slip into a serious violation — fines, and in bad cases, the kind of trouble that ends careers.

**The fix:** treat every odometer disclosure as if a regulator is reading it, because one might be. Record the exact mileage. Mark the correct status (actual, not actual, exceeds mechanical limits). File it. Every time, no exceptions.

## Mistake 4: Keeping bad records (or none)

Your state requires you to keep deal files — titles, disclosures, bills of sale, tax records, temporary tag logs — for a set number of years. New dealers throw receipts in a drawer and figure they'll organize it "later."

Later arrives as an audit notice, and now they're reconstructing a year of transactions from memory and crumpled paper. I've watched it happen. It's ugly, and it can cost you your license if you can't produce what's required.

**The fix:** set up a clean, boring filing system on **day one** — a folder per deal, digital or physical, with a checklist of what goes in each. It takes five minutes per sale and saves you a nightmare later.

## Mistake 5: Letting the bond or insurance lapse

Your surety bond and your insurance are **conditions of your license.** Let either lapse and you're not just uninsured — you're operating **illegally** until it's reinstated, and your license can be suspended.

Renewals sneak up on people. The bond renews annually, the insurance on its own cycle, the license on another. Miss one and you're in violation without even realizing it.

**The fix:** put every renewal — license, bond, insurance — on a calendar with a reminder **30 days early.** Set it once. This single habit prevents an entire category of disasters.

## Mistake 6: Lying or omitting on the application

Some folks with a blemish on their record — an old conviction, a past business issue — try to leave it off the application, hoping it won't come up.

It comes up. States run background checks. And here's the cruel irony: **the omission is usually worse than the thing being omitted.** A disclosed old issue is often approved. A *hidden* one, discovered later, reads as fraud and gets you denied — or revoked after the fact.

**The fix:** disclose everything, honestly, the first time. If you're worried about a specific item, call the licensing office and ask how they handle it. Transparency almost always beats concealment.

## Mistake 7: Buying inventory you can't appraise

Auctions are intoxicating. Cars rolling through, prices flashing, the pressure to bid. New dealers overpay, buy cars with hidden problems, or blow their whole budget in one afternoon on stuff they can't sell for a profit.

**The fix:** go slow at first. Attend an auction and buy *nothing* the first time — just watch. Learn what things sell for. Buy a few conservative, easy-to-resell cars before you swing for the fences. Your appraisal eye is a muscle; it needs reps before you trust it with real money.

## Mistake 8: Pricing on emotion instead of the market

Two flavors: pricing too high because you "have money in it," or too low because a car's making you nervous. Both bleed money. The market doesn't care what you paid, and a car that sits for an extra two months quietly costs you far more than the discount that would've moved it.

**The fix:** price to what comparable cars are actually *selling* for locally, respect the clock, and be willing to take a small, fast loss on a loser instead of a big, slow one. Turn is your friend.

## Mistake 9: Treating customers like one-time marks

The used-car business carries a reputation, and every dealer who cuts corners feeds it. In the age of online reviews, that reputation is now *searchable.* One furious, wronged customer can cost you more future business than any single sale ever made you.

**The fix:** disclose known issues, honor reasonable expectations, and treat people the way you'd want your own family treated buying a car. It's not just ethics — it's the cheapest, most durable marketing you have.

## The pattern behind all nine

Look back at the list and you'll notice something: almost every mistake comes from **rushing or assuming.** Signing before checking. Budgeting for the paper, not the business. Filing "later." Assuming the background check won't catch it. Assuming the car's worth what you hoped.

The dealers who avoid these aren't smarter or luckier. They just slow down at the exact moments everyone else speeds up: before signing, before buying, before filing. Make one free phone call, keep one boring folder, set one calendar reminder, and you've sidestepped most of the ways this goes wrong.

That's the whole secret. Slow is smooth, smooth is profitable.
