The first thing that many people ask is, how much does it cost to get an independent dealer license?

The trouble is, the license application price is just fraction of the total cost of starting up.

A new dealer will also require company registration, a surety bond, insurance, education, fingerprints, commercial rent, zoning permissions, signs, dealer plates, office equipment, inventory and operating capital.

So the honest answer is not a number.

It’s a budget.

## 1. Application Fee for Dealer License

Application fees vary by state.

In California, the cost of the application fee for a car dealer is $175 and is non-refundable. Additional fees can apply for dealer plates, branches and endorsements.

Other states have distinct fee regimes.

The main lesson is that a $175 or $500 application cost doesn't imply you can create a dealership for $175 or $500.

## 3. Fee for Surety Bond

Many states demand a dealer surety bond .

Some instances now:

* Texas: $50,000 bond for most relevant independent GDN categories
* Georgia: Bond $35,000
* Washington: $30,000 bail
* Indiana: $25,000 bond

These are bond amounts, not necessarily what you will pay.

You will usually pay a fee to the surety business.

The premium may be based on:

* Credit:
* Business history
* AmountBond
* Previous reports
* Underwriting

## 4. Insurance Expenses

Dealer insurance may cover:

* Garage liability coverage
* Commercial vehicle
* General liability
* Garagekeepers
* Dealer open lot
* Workers' Compensation (WC)

Some states demand evidence of insurance as part of licensure.

For example, Georgia requires eligible used dealer applicants to provide proof of garage liability insurance.

Insurance price is based on real business, not terms like “dealer license”.

## 5. Dealer training

Some states mandate training.

Possible costs:

* Exam
* Traveling
* Course Fee
* Later further education

Some states (California, Texas, Georgia, Indiana, Oregon, etc.) impose education requirements for some candidates.

## 6. Background Checks and Fingerprinting

More owners or cops might imply more expenses for fingerprints.

This is generally not the largest expense, but it is part of the initial budget.

## 7. Location of Dealership

This is the biggest licensing fee for many dealers.

## 9. Symptoms

A compliance sign may range from a modest sum up into the thousands depending on size, materials, illumination, installation and local sign standards.

First, choose the company name.

You don’t want to have to pay for the same sign twice as a branding approach.

## Difference Between Landed Cost and Cost Price

New dealers usually work out profit in this way:

* Car 8000 is for sale.
* Car for sale, $11,000.
* Profit = $3,000

The actual dealership calculation is probably closer to:

* Purchase price: $8,000.
* Buyer fee at auction: $650
* Transportation: $250
* Inspection: $100.00
* Tires $500
* Detail: $175.00
* Key: $ 225
* Minor repairs 600 $

## **Actual cost of investment: $10,500**

It sells for $11,000, although the seeming $3,000 margin is really closer to $500, after overhead.

And that’s why license cost is only the first financial issue.

## How Much Stock Should You Buy?

Don't blow all your cash on vehicles.

If you have $50,000 cash to start your business, and you spend $49,000 on inventory, you may not have the money to repair, move, insure, or advertize it.

The more reasonable approach would be to allocate funds among:

* Shares
* Re-conditioning
* Operating reserve
* Marketing
* Licensing

The precise proportion varies by your dealership.

The concept is simple: cash is part of your inventory strategy.

## Questions to Ask Before Spending $1

* Do operations need this?
* Is there a state-mandated form or provider?
* Can I receive many quotes?
* Is there a way to postpone this charge till it is approved?
* Is there a monthly fee associated with this purchase?
* And so what if the application for the license is denied?

Those seven questions might help you secure your startup funding.

## Another Budget Rule

Include a contingency reserve in your business strategy.

Used-car companies have several expenditures that are impossible to estimate correctly before opening: a car requires an unexpected catalytic converter, a transporter charges for storage, a sign has to be redone, or the state wants another paperwork.

A contingency reserve doesn’t make errors painless, but it avoids one little surprise from interrupting the whole launch.

For a new dealer, liquidity protection is frequently more crucial than purchasing one extra car in the first month.
