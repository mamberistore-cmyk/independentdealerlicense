Floor plan financing is a revolving line of credit made specifically for buying dealership inventory. It lets you stock more cars than your cash alone would allow — you borrow to buy, sell the car, repay the principal plus fees, and repeat. Used well it is rocket fuel; used carelessly it is how lots go under. Here is how it works and how to use it safely.

## Quick answer

Floor plan (or "floorplan") financing is a credit line for inventory. You draw on it to buy cars, and each car accrues fees and interest until it sells. It expands your buying power but eats margin the longer a car sits — so it rewards fast turnover, not hoarding.

## How it works

The cycle is simple:

1. You get approved for a floor-plan line with a lender.
2. You **draw** on it to buy a car (often at auction).
3. The car sits on your lot, **accruing interest and fees**.
4. You **sell** the car and **repay** the principal plus costs.
5. You repeat with the freed-up credit.

It lets a cash-strapped dealer fill a lot instead of buying one car at a time. Pair it with a realistic [startup budget](/blog/how-much-does-an-independent-dealer-license-cost) so the payments fit your cash flow.

## What it costs

Floor plan is not free money. Expect:

- **Interest** on each drawn amount.
- **Curtailment** payments (partial principal paydowns) if a car sits too long.
- **Fees** per unit or per draw.

Every day a floor-planned car sits, it is eating your margin. That clock is the whole reason to price and sell quickly.

## Using it safely

My rule: **floor plan is a tool for turning inventory fast, not for hoarding it.** A few guardrails:

- Only floor-plan cars you expect to **sell quickly**.
- Watch your **aging** — the longer a car sits, the more it costs.
- Keep some **cash reserve**; do not rely 100% on the line.
- If a car is not moving, the financing clock is a reason to **reprice**, not wait.

## A real example

Ben floor-plans six cars to fill his lot. Four sell within a month — great. But two sit for 90 days, quietly racking up interest and a curtailment payment, turning small expected profits into losses. Had he repriced them at day 45 instead of waiting for his dream buyer, the line would have worked in his favor.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Hoarding inventory** on the line instead of turning it.
- **Ignoring aging** and curtailment costs.
- **Going all-in** with no cash reserve.
- **Waiting for a perfect buyer** while fees pile up.

## Tips

- Track each unit's **days on lot** and its financing cost.
- Set a reprice trigger (e.g., 45 days) and follow it.
- Keep a cash cushion outside the line.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is floor plan financing worth it?

It can be, if you turn inventory fast. Slow-selling cars make it expensive.

### What is a curtailment?

A required partial principal paydown when a floor-planned car sits beyond a set period.

### Do I need floor plan to start?

No. Many dealers start on cash and add a line later as they scale.

## Conclusion

Floor plan financing can turn a thin bankroll into a full lot — but only if you respect the clock. Borrow to **turn** inventory, not to hoard it; watch aging and fees; keep a cash reserve; and reprice slow cars instead of waiting. Treat the line as a fast-moving tool and it multiplies your business. Treat it as free money and it multiplies your losses.
