Insurance is the part of dealer licensing where I've seen the most money wasted — in both directions. Some dealers buy the bare minimum, get burned by a claim, and lose everything. Others get talked into a stack of coverage they'll never use by a broker working on commission. The trick is knowing which policies are load-bearing and which are optional, and I'm going to lay that out plainly.

First, the disclaimer I'll repeat forever: **your surety bond is not insurance.** If you're fuzzy on that, read the bond guide first. The bond protects your customers from you. Insurance protects *you* from the chaos of running a lot. Different tools, both required.

## The one you absolutely must have: Garage Liability

If you take away one thing, take this: **garage liability insurance is the core policy for a car dealer**, and most states require proof of it before they'll issue your license.

Garage liability covers **bodily injury and property damage** arising from your dealership operations. Someone slips on your lot. A test-drive turns into a fender bender. A car you're moving clips a parked vehicle. That's garage liability territory.

Two things to understand about it:

- It's specifically designed for **automotive businesses.** A generic business liability policy usually won't satisfy your state, because it excludes the auto operations that are your entire business.
- Your state will specify **minimum limits** — often something like $50,000 or more per occurrence, sometimes higher. Meet the state minimum, but seriously consider going above it. The minimum is the floor, not a recommendation.

This is not the policy to skimp on. One at-fault injury during a test drive can generate a claim that dwarfs a lifetime of premiums.

## The one that protects customer cars: Garagekeepers

**Garagekeepers coverage** protects vehicles that belong to *customers* while they're in your care — being serviced, stored, or held on your lot. If you're a straight retail lot that doesn't do repairs or hold customer cars, you may not strictly need it. If you do any service work or store vehicles for people, you want it.

It usually comes in flavors — legal liability (you only pay if you're at fault) versus direct/broad coverage. Ask your broker to explain which you're getting, because "garagekeepers" on the quote doesn't tell you the trigger.

## The one for your inventory: Dealer's Open Lot / Physical Damage

Your surety bond won't replace a hailed-out lot. Garage liability won't either — liability covers damage you cause to *others,* not your own inventory.

**Dealer's open lot coverage** (also called inventory or physical damage coverage) protects **the cars you own and are trying to sell** against theft, fire, hail, flood, and vandalism. If you've got $80,000 of inventory sitting on a lot and a hailstorm rolls through, this is the policy standing between you and a very bad month.

It's typically not *state-mandated,* but if you carry real inventory value, going without it is a gamble I wouldn't take. Talk to your broker about how it's rated — often on your average inventory value with a per-vehicle limit.

## Dealer plate / drive-away coverage

Those dealer plates let you drive inventory around. But a plate is not insurance. You need coverage that follows the **plate and driver** when a dealer-plated car is on the road — for you, your staff, and customers on test drives.

Sometimes this is folded into your garage policy; sometimes it's a rider. The point is to confirm — explicitly — that a car on your dealer plate is covered when someone's driving it, and *who* counts as an insured driver. Don't assume. Ask the question and get the answer in writing.

## The optional stuff (buy if it fits, skip if it doesn't)

Here's where brokers earn their commission and where you should think for yourself:

- **Commercial property** — covers your building/office and its contents. Sensible if you own or lease a real structure with equipment inside.
- **Workers' compensation** — often *legally required* the moment you have employees, so this can jump from "optional" to "mandatory" fast. Confirm your state's employee threshold.
- **Business interruption** — replaces income if a covered disaster shuts you down. Nice to have, not essential for a lean startup.
- **Cyber / data** — only relevant if you're storing a lot of customer financial data digitally. Most tiny lots can pass at first.
- **Umbrella policy** — extra liability limits stacked on top of your other coverage. Worth considering once you have real assets to protect.

None of these are scams — they're legitimate coverages. But you don't need all of them on day one. Buy what matches your actual operation, and revisit annually as you grow.

## What a good insurance setup looks like for a small lot

If you're a modest independent retail dealer just getting going, a sensible starting stack is usually:

1. **Garage liability** at or above your state's minimum limits. *(Required.)*
2. **Dealer plate / drive-away** coverage confirmed in writing. *(Required in practice.)*
3. **Dealer's open lot** coverage sized to your inventory value. *(Strongly recommended.)*
4. **Garagekeepers** *if* you hold or service customer vehicles.
5. **Workers' comp** the moment you cross your state's employee threshold.

That's a real, defensible setup — not the bare legal minimum, not an over-sold fortress.

## How to buy it without getting fleeced

**Use a broker who actually knows dealers.** Automotive insurance is a specialty. A broker who mostly writes homeowner policies will either exclude the wrong things or over-cover you out of caution. Find someone who writes dealer policies all day.

**Get three quotes.** I say this in every guide because it's true in every category. Garage liability rates swing hard between carriers for identical coverage.

**Read the exclusions, not just the limits.** The limit tells you the ceiling; the exclusions tell you when the policy simply won't respond. That's where the nasty surprises live. If test drives or "permissive drivers" are excluded, you have a problem hiding in plain sight.

**Match coverage to reality, then re-check yearly.** Buy for the lot you have now. When inventory grows or you hire your first employee, revisit — those milestones change what you legally and practically need.

## The bottom line

The state cares mostly about **garage liability**, and rightly so — it's the coverage that protects the public. But protecting *your* business means thinking a step further: your inventory, your plated cars, your customers' vehicles. Cover those honestly, skip the upsells that don't fit your operation, and read the exclusions before you sign.

Insurance is boring right up until the day it's the only thing standing between you and disaster. On that day, the dealers who read the fine print sleep fine. The ones who bought the cheapest quote on the page do not.
