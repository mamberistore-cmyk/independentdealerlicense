The difference comes down to **who you are allowed to sell to**. A **retail dealer license** lets you sell vehicles to the general public. A **wholesale dealer license** only lets you sell to other licensed dealers — never directly to consumers. If your plan is to run a lot and sell to everyday buyers, you need the retail license.

Here is how to tell them apart and choose correctly the first time.

## Quick answer

- **Retail license:** sell to the public. Requires a customer-facing lot, signage, and usually stricter rules.
- **Wholesale license:** sell only to other dealers (often through auctions). Lighter location requirements, lower overhead, but no public sales.

Pick wholesale only if you truly never plan to sell to consumers.

## Who you can sell to

This is the core distinction. A wholesale dealer moves inventory between dealers and at dealer-only auctions. They never advertise to the public or sell a car to a retail customer. A retail dealer does the opposite — their whole business is selling to individual buyers.

Selling to the public on a wholesale-only license is a serious violation that can cost you the license, so the line matters.

## How requirements differ

### Location

Retail licenses usually require a proper **display lot** with customer parking, posted hours, and permanent signage. Wholesale licenses often have **lighter location rules** because no customers visit — sometimes even a modest office qualifies. Always confirm with your state.

### Cost and overhead

Because the location bar is lower, wholesale is typically cheaper to run. Both still require a **surety bond** and insurance. For the full money picture, see our [dealer license cost breakdown](/blog/how-much-does-an-independent-dealer-license-cost).

### Day-to-day business

Retail means marketing, test drives, financing conversations, and consumer-protection paperwork. Wholesale is a quieter, dealer-to-dealer flow built around auctions and relationships.

## A real example

Marcus buys cars cheaply at auction and wants to resell them for profit to the public. He is tempted by a "cheap wholesale license," but that would forbid the retail sales his whole model depends on. He instead follows his state's [retail dealer application steps](/blog/how-to-get-independent-dealer-license) and gets the correct license.

His cousin Lena, on the other hand, only wants to move cars between dealers as a side business. For her, the wholesale license is perfect and keeps her overhead low.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Buying a wholesale license to save money, then selling to the public.** That is illegal and risks your license.
- **Assuming location rules are the same.** They are usually different — verify before you lease.
- **Skipping the bond or insurance** because wholesale "feels smaller." Both are still required.

## Tips

- Write down your business model in one sentence, then match the license to it.
- If you will *ever* sell to the public, get the retail license from the start.
- Ask your state directly which license covers dealer-auction buying — rules vary.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a wholesale dealer sell to the public?

No. Wholesale-only dealers may sell to other licensed dealers, not to retail customers.

### Is a wholesale license cheaper?

Usually yes, because the location requirements are lighter. You still need a surety bond and insurance.

### Can I hold both licenses?

Some states let dealers hold or upgrade licenses; others treat them separately. Check your state's specific classes.

## Conclusion

Choose **retail** if you will sell to everyday buyers, and **wholesale** only if you will strictly sell to other dealers. The retail license costs a bit more and demands a real lot, but it is the only legal path to selling cars to the public. Confirm your state's exact rules, then apply for the one that matches how you actually plan to make money.
