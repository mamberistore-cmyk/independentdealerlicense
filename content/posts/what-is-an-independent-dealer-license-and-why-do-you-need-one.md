Let me start with the question I got asked at least once a week when I worked the licensing window: *"I sold a couple of cars last year and made decent money. Do I really need one of these things?"*

The honest answer is almost always yes — and the people asking usually don't like hearing it. So let's back up and talk about what this license actually is, why it exists, and where the line sits between a guy who sells his old truck and a person the state considers a dealer.

## What an independent dealer license actually is

An **independent dealer license** is a permit from your state that legally allows you to buy and sell vehicles as a business — specifically *used* vehicles, without being tied to a manufacturer franchise. "Independent" is the key word. A franchise dealer sells new Fords or Hondas under a factory agreement. An independent dealer buys and sells pre-owned inventory from wherever they can find it: auctions, trade-ins, private sellers, other dealers.

The license does a few things at once:

- It makes you a **legal business** in the eyes of the DMV, so you can title and transfer vehicles in a dealer capacity.
- It lets you buy at **dealer-only auctions**, which is where a huge share of used inventory actually comes from.
- It gives you **dealer plates**, so you can move and demo inventory without registering each car individually.
- It authorizes you to issue **temporary tags** and handle titling for your customers.

Think of it less as a piece of paper and more as a key that unlocks the entire wholesale side of the car business — the part regular people never see.

## Why the license exists at all

People assume licensing is just a way for the state to collect fees. There's some truth to that, but the real reason is older and more practical: **the used-car business used to be the Wild West, and consumers got burned constantly.**

Rolled-back odometers. Salvage cars sold as clean. Title washing. Sellers who took a deposit and vanished. Licensing is the state's answer. In exchange for the privilege of doing this as a business, you agree to keep records, disclose odometer readings honestly, post a surety bond, and answer to a regulator if you cheat someone.

I've sat across from customers who'd been genuinely defrauded, and the bond behind a licensed dealer is sometimes the only reason they got a dime back. When I hear "why do I need all this red tape," I think about those folks. The tape exists because people got hurt.

## The magic number: how many cars makes you a "dealer"?

Here's the part everyone's really asking about. Every state sets a threshold — a number of vehicles you can sell in a **12-month period** before the law considers you a dealer who needs a license. Sell your own personal car? Fine. Sell your spouse's car and an old motorcycle? Also fine. But once you cross the line, you need a license.

That threshold varies, but a lot of states land somewhere around **3 to 6 vehicles per year.** Some are stricter. The exact count matters less than the principle: **if you're buying vehicles with the intent to resell them for profit, you're a dealer** — even if you only flip two, because intent counts too.

> The tell isn't the number, it's the pattern. If the titles never sit in your name for long, and money's the point, a regulator sees a dealer. Full stop.

## What is "curbstoning," and why should you care?

Selling cars for profit without a license has a name in the industry: **curbstoning.** It's called that because the classic move is parking cars along a curb or in a lot you don't own, with a phone number in the window.

States take curbstoning seriously, and enforcement has gotten sharper. Investigators trawl online marketplaces looking for the same phone number attached to a dozen listings, or the same seller "just helping a friend" over and over. Penalties range from fines that dwarf the cost of a license to, in repeat cases, criminal charges.

So the calculus is simple. The license costs you some money and a few weeks. Getting caught curbstoning costs you more money, potentially a criminal record, and every future opportunity in the business. It's not a close call.

## The upside nobody mentions

We've talked about the "you have to" side. Let's talk about the "you get to" side, because the license is genuinely valuable.

**Auction access alone can pay for the license.** Dealer-only auctions are where inventory is cheapest. The spread between what you pay at a dealer auction and what a private buyer pays retail is your entire business model. Without a license, that door is locked.

**You can move cars efficiently.** Dealer plates mean you're not registering and de-registering every vehicle you touch. You can demo, transport, and hold inventory like an actual business.

**You look legitimate — because you are.** Customers are wary of buying a used car from a stranger. A licensed dealer with a real location and a bond behind them closes deals a curbstoner never could. Legitimacy is a sales tool.

## So do *you* need one?

Run yourself through three quick questions:

1. **Are you buying vehicles primarily to resell them?** If yes, you're heading toward dealer territory.
2. **Will you cross your state's annual sales threshold?** If you're doing this seriously, you will — fast.
3. **Do you want auction access and dealer plates?** If yes, the license isn't a burden, it's the whole point.

If you answered yes to any of those, the license isn't optional bureaucracy — it's the foundation of doing this the right way, the profitable way, and the way that doesn't end with an investigator at your door.

The good news? It's more approachable than that intimidating stack of forms suggests. Once you understand *what* the license is and *why* it exists, the how-to stops feeling like a maze and starts feeling like a to-do list. And a to-do list, you can knock out.
