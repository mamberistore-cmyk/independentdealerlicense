Getting the license is the easy part. I know that sounds backwards after everything I've written about bonds and inspections, but it's true. The license is a finite checklist with a clear finish line. *Running a dealership that makes money* is an open-ended puzzle you solve every single day. So let's talk about the business itself — the part that determines whether you're still open in two years.

## Start with brutally honest numbers

Before the romance of picking out cars, do the math. A used-car lot makes money on the **spread** — the gap between what you pay for a car and what you sell it for — minus every cost of holding and selling it.

Those costs are sneakier than new dealers expect:

- **Reconditioning** — cleaning, minor repairs, safety fixes to make a car sellable.
- **Holding cost** — every day a car sits, it's tying up money and (if financed) accruing interest.
- **Overhead** — rent, insurance, utilities, your time.
- **The occasional loser** — the car that needs a transmission you didn't catch, or that sits for ninety days and sells at a loss.

If your average spread doesn't comfortably cover all of that with profit left over, you don't have a business — you have an expensive hobby. Run these numbers *before* you sign anything.

## Write the plan (even a one-pager)

You don't need a 40-page business plan with hockey-stick graphs. You need honest answers to a handful of questions:

1. **What will you sell?** Economy commuters under $10k? Trucks and SUVs? A specialty like classic cars or work vans? Focus beats "a little of everything."
2. **Who's your buyer?** Budget-first families? Subprime buyers who need financing? Enthusiasts? Your inventory, pricing, and location should all point at one answer.
3. **Where's your inventory coming from?** (More on this below.)
4. **How will people find you?** Marketplace listings, a simple website, word of mouth, a busy road?
5. **What's your runway?** How many months can you cover costs before the lot has to sustain itself?

Write it down. Not for a bank — for you. The act of writing it exposes the fuzzy thinking.

## Finding inventory: where the cars actually come from

This is the skill that separates dealers who thrive from dealers who scrape by. Your main sources:

**Dealer-only auctions.** The big one, and a major reason you got licensed. Physical and online auctions sell wholesale to licensed dealers. Prices are lower, selection is huge — but so is the risk, because you're often buying fast with limited inspection. Start conservative. Buy a few, learn the rhythm, don't blow your capital on week one.

**Trade-ins.** Once you're selling, you'll take trades. A well-bought trade can be your best-margin car because you controlled the acquisition price.

**Private-party purchases.** Buying from the public can score great deals, but it's time-intensive and requires a good eye.

**Other dealers and wholesalers.** Sometimes another dealer has a car that's wrong for their lot but perfect for yours. Networking pays.

The core skill underneath all of these is **appraisal** — knowing what a car is truly worth reconditioned and retailed, and what it'll cost to get there. That skill is earned by doing it, making mistakes, and paying attention. Budget to be mediocre at it for a while.

## Floor-plan financing: rocket fuel or rope

Most cash-strapped new dealers can't buy enough inventory to fill a lot. **Floor-plan financing** solves that — it's a revolving line of credit specifically for buying inventory. You borrow to buy cars, sell them, pay back the principal plus fees, and repeat.

Used well, it lets you stock more cars and turn more deals than your cash alone allows. Used carelessly, it's how lots go under — because those fees and interest accrue whether the car sells or not. Every day a floor-planned car sits, it's eating your margin.

My rule: **floor-plan is a tool for turning inventory fast, not for hoarding it.** If a car isn't moving, the financing clock is a reason to reprice and sell, not to wait for your dream buyer.

## Pricing so cars actually move

New dealers make two opposite mistakes: they price too high out of greed, or too low out of fear. Both hurt.

- **Price to the market, not to your cost.** The market doesn't care what you paid. Check what comparable cars are actually *selling* for (not just listed at) in your area.
- **Respect the clock.** A car priced $500 too high that sits for 60 extra days costs you far more than $500 in holding, interest, and opportunity. Turn beats squeeze.
- **Be willing to cut a loser loose.** The hardest discipline in this business is taking a small loss quickly instead of a big loss slowly. The car that's been on the lot 90 days is telling you something. Listen.

## The paperwork that keeps you licensed

Selling the car is the fun part. The paperwork is what keeps your license. Every state requires you to keep meticulous records:

- Title assignments and transfers.
- **Odometer disclosures** (federally required, taken very seriously).
- Bills of sale and buyer's guides.
- Sales tax collected and remitted.
- Temporary tag logs.

Set up a clean filing system on **day one,** before you have a single deal to file. Retrofitting organization onto a shoebox of receipts during an audit is a special kind of misery I've watched people endure. Don't be them.

## Marketing without a big budget

You don't need a Super Bowl ad. You need to be **findable** and **trustworthy.**

- **List everywhere buyers look.** Online marketplaces are where used cars sell today. Good photos and honest descriptions outperform fancy branding.
- **Photos matter more than you think.** Clean the car, shoot it in good light, show the flaws honestly. Buyers trust the listing that shows the scratch.
- **A simple website** with your inventory and a phone number builds legitimacy for almost nothing.
- **Reputation compounds.** Reviews and word of mouth are the cheapest, most powerful marketing a small lot has. One furious customer online can cost you more than any ad ever earned. Treat people right — it's also good business.

## The unglamorous truth about the first year

Your first year, you will overpay for a car. You'll get stuck with one that won't sell. You'll misjudge a repair. Everyone does. The dealers who make it aren't the ones who avoid every mistake — they're the ones who **keep enough capital in reserve to survive the mistakes** and learn from them.

So start lean. Keep a cushion. Buy conservatively until your appraisal eye sharpens. Turn inventory instead of falling in love with it. And treat the boring stuff — records, pricing discipline, honest listings — as seriously as the fun stuff.

The license got you in the door. The business is what you build once you're inside. Build it patiently, and a gravel corner lot with a double-wide trailer can turn into something real. I know, because that's exactly where mine started.
