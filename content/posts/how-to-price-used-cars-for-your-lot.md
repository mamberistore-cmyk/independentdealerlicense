Pricing is where new dealers quietly lose money. Price too high out of greed and the car sits, racking up holding costs; price too low out of fear and you give away margin. The fix is simple in principle: **price to the market, respect the clock, and be willing to cut a loser loose.** Here is how to do it in practice.

## Quick answer

Price each car to what comparable vehicles are actually **selling** for in your area, not to what you paid or hope to get. Watch days-on-lot, and reprice or move slow cars before holding costs eat the profit. Turn beats squeeze.

## Price to the market, not to your cost

The market does not care what you paid. Your cost tells you your **floor**, not your price. To set a real price:

1. Find **comparable** cars (same make, model, year, mileage, condition) nearby.
2. Look at what they **sold** for, not just their list prices.
3. Position your car competitively within that range.

If you buy well at [auction](/blog/how-to-buy-cars-at-dealer-auctions), you will have room to price competitively and still profit.

## Respect the clock

Every day a car sits, it costs you — in holding, in financing (if it is floor-planned), and in opportunity. A car priced $500 too high that sits an extra 60 days can cost you far more than the $500 that would have moved it. Track **days on lot** for every unit and treat aging as a signal.

## Cut losers loose

The hardest discipline in this business is taking a **small loss quickly** instead of a big loss slowly. A car that has sat 90 days is telling you something — the price, the market, or the car is wrong. Reprice decisively, or wholesale it and free the cash for a car that will actually turn.

## A real example

Dana buys a sedan and prices it $800 above the local selling range because she "has money in it." It sits. Sixty days later she has paid extra holding and financing costs and finally drops the price anyway — netting less than if she had priced it right on day one. Her rule now: price to the market immediately, and reprice at day 45.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Pricing to your cost** instead of the market.
- **Falling in love** with a car and refusing to reprice.
- **Ignoring days-on-lot** until the loss is large.
- **Comparing to list prices** instead of actual sold prices.

## Tips

- Set a **reprice trigger** (e.g., 45 days) and follow it.
- Track every car's days on lot and holding cost.
- Take the small, fast loss over the big, slow one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I price based on what I paid?

No. Your cost is your floor. Price to what comparable cars actually sell for locally.

### How long should a car sit before I reprice?

Many dealers reprice around 30–45 days. The exact trigger matters less than actually following it.

### Is it better to hold out for full price?

Rarely. Holding costs usually erase the extra margin. Turn beats squeeze.

## Conclusion

Good pricing is not about squeezing the most out of one car — it is about **turning inventory** at a healthy margin, again and again. Price to the market, watch the clock, and cut losers loose without drama. Do that consistently and your lot stays liquid, your cash keeps working, and your profit comes from volume and discipline instead of hope.
