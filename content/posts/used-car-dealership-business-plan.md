You do not need a 40-page business plan to open a used-car lot. You need honest answers to a handful of questions: what you will sell, who buys it, where inventory comes from, how the money works, and how people find you. This guide walks through a simple, one-page plan you can actually use.

## Quick answer

A useful dealership plan answers five things: your **niche**, your **buyer**, your **inventory source**, your **budget and pricing**, and your **marketing**. Write it on one page, be honest with the numbers, and revisit it as you learn.

## Start with the money math

A used-car lot makes money on the **spread** — the gap between what you pay for a car and what you sell it for — minus every cost of holding and selling it: reconditioning, holding time, overhead, and the occasional loser. If your average spread does not comfortably cover all of that with profit left over, you have an expensive hobby, not a business. Run these numbers before anything else, and pair them with a realistic [startup budget](/blog/how-much-does-an-independent-dealer-license-cost).

## The five questions

### 1. What will you sell?

Pick a focus: budget commuters, trucks and SUVs, or a specialty. Focus beats "a little of everything."

### 2. Who is your buyer?

Budget-first families? Subprime buyers who need financing? Enthusiasts? Your inventory and pricing should point at one answer.

### 3. Where does inventory come from?

Mostly dealer-only auctions, trade-ins, and private-party buys. (Getting [licensed](/blog/how-to-get-independent-dealer-license) is what unlocks the auctions.)

### 4. What is your budget and pricing?

Know your working capital, your per-car target margin, and how you will price to the market — not to your cost.

### 5. How will people find you?

Online marketplace listings, a simple website, a busy road, and word of mouth.

## A real example

Dana writes one page: she will sell reliable commuter cars under $12,000 to budget-conscious families, source them at a nearby dealer auction, keep $20,000 working capital, target a $1,500 gross per car, and list everywhere online with honest photos. That page keeps her focused when a flashy truck tempts her off-strategy at the auction.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Selling a bit of everything.** No focus, no repeat buyers.
- **Pricing to your cost** instead of the market.
- **Spending all capital on inventory** with no reserve.
- **Skipping the money math** at the start.

## Tips

- Keep the plan to **one page** — a living document, not a binder.
- Track every car: purchase, fees, recon, resale, profit.
- Reserve cash for slow weeks and surprises.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a formal business plan to get licensed?

Usually not for the license itself, but you absolutely need one for your own sanity and cash flow.

### How much working capital do I need?

Enough for a few cars plus a reserve — many start in the $10,000–$30,000 range, or use floor-plan financing.

### What is the most common reason lots fail?

Poor money discipline — overpaying for inventory and running out of cash.

## Conclusion

A used-car dealership plan does not need to be fancy — it needs to be **honest**. Nail your niche, your buyer, your sourcing, your numbers, and your marketing on a single page, and you will make better decisions than a rival with a thick binder and fuzzy math. Start lean, track everything, and let the plan evolve as your lot teaches you what actually sells.
