Most states let you sell somewhere around **3 to 6 vehicles in a 12-month period** before you are considered a dealer who needs a license — but the exact number is only half the story. The other half is **intent**: if you are buying cars specifically to resell for profit, you can be treated as a dealer even below the count. Here is how the rule really works.

## Quick answer

There is no single national number, but many states draw the line around **3–6 vehicles per year**. Selling your own personal cars is fine. Buying cars to flip for profit makes you a dealer — get licensed before you cross the line.

## Why the number is only half the rule

States care about two things: **how many** vehicles you sell, and **why**. Selling your old car and your spouse's car in the same year is clearly personal. But buying vehicles with the plan to resell them for profit signals a **business**, and regulators can treat you as an unlicensed dealer even at a low count.

> The tell is the pattern: titles that never rest in your name for long, and profit as the point.

## What counts (and what usually doesn't)

Typically **counts** toward the threshold:

- Vehicles you bought to resell.
- A repeating pattern of quick buy-and-sell.

Usually **does not** count:

- Selling a vehicle titled in your name that you actually used.
- An occasional genuine personal sale.

Always confirm your state's exact definition — the specifics vary.

## The risk of guessing wrong: curbstoning

Selling cars for profit without a license is called **curbstoning**, and enforcement has gotten smarter — investigators watch online marketplaces for the same seller or phone number moving car after car. Penalties can dwarf the cost of a license, and repeat cases can bring criminal charges. If you are anywhere near the line, it is cheaper and safer to get [properly licensed](/blog/how-to-get-independent-dealer-license).

## A real example

Alex flips two cars in three months and figures he is safely "under the limit." But both titles moved through his name quickly and he advertised them for profit. To a regulator, that is a pattern, not a coincidence. Alex would have been far safer getting licensed before the second flip — the [license pays for itself](/blog/how-much-does-an-independent-dealer-license-cost) through auction access alone.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Focusing only on the number** and ignoring intent.
- **Assuming other states' limits** apply to yours.
- **Reusing one phone number** across many listings — a classic curbstoning flag.

## Tips

- If money is the point, assume you need a license.
- Check your state's exact threshold and definition of "dealer."
- When in doubt, get licensed — auction access usually justifies it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a national limit?

No. Each state sets its own threshold, commonly in the 3–6 vehicle range per year.

### Can I be a dealer below the number?

Yes. If you are clearly buying to resell for profit, intent alone can make you a dealer.

### What happens if I curbstone?

Fines that often exceed the license cost, and in repeat cases, criminal charges.

## Conclusion

The number matters, but **intent matters more**. If you are selling your own vehicles occasionally, you are fine. If you are buying cars to flip for profit, you are a dealer in the eyes of the law — so get licensed before you cross the line. It is cheaper than a curbstoning penalty, and it unlocks the auctions that make the business work.
